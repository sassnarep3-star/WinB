Basket or oven-style air fryer: what should you compare?

Two machines, one word on the box. The differences that decide whether it fits your cooking are almost never the ones printed on the front.

By Counterwise editorial Updated 2026-11-20 Reading time 7 min Basis published specifications and public guidance

The short answer

A basket air fryer is a deep, enclosed drawer with a fan above it. An oven-style air fryer is a small convection oven with a door. Both circulate hot air; almost everything that differs follows from the shape of the cavity.

Basket models heat up faster and are easier to shake mid-cook. Oven-style models hold more at once, take flat or wide items, and can usually do more than fry. If you cook for two and want speed, a basket. If you cook for four or want one box to replace a toaster oven, an oven-style.

Compare usable area, not litres

Capacity in quarts is volume, and volume is not the number that matters. A deep 6-quart basket and a shallow 6-quart oven hold very different dinners. What matters is the usable floor area and the headroom above it.

Measure the largest thing you actually cook. A whole chicken, a pizza, a tray of vegetables for four, four slices of toast. Then check the interior dimensions of the machine, not its capacity figure. This is the single most common return reason and the least visible number in a listing.

Two baskets versus one big one

A dual-basket model buys you different temperatures and different finish times at once. That is genuinely useful if you regularly cook a protein and a side together. It costs you: two 3-quart drawers hold less than one 6-quart drawer, because each has its own walls.

A single large basket holds wider, flatter things that dual baskets cannot. If you mostly cook one thing at a time for a family, one big basket is the better shape. If you cook two things and hate timing, two baskets are worth the lost width.

The controls you will actually touch

Preset counts are a marketing number. Most people use two settings and a temperature dial. What matters is whether the temperature range is adjustable in useful steps, whether there is a preheat, and whether the timer is easy to change mid-cook.

Sync-finish exists on dual-basket machines so two drawers land at the same moment. It is genuinely useful if you use both baskets. It is dead weight if you do not.

Cleaning, honestly

Every model claims dishwasher-safe parts. The part that fails first is rarely the basket floor; it is the rim, the hinge, and the gap where the drawer slides. Look for how many separate pieces come out and whether any of them has a non-stick coating that a dishwasher will dull.

Oven-style models have more interior surface and usually a heating element that food can reach. Basket models put the element out of reach, which is easier to clean and harder to preheat evenly.

Counter space and clearance

Basket models need vertical clearance above them because the drawer pulls out and the vent is on top. Oven-style models need horizontal clearance in front for the door swing and often more depth than a basket of the same capacity.

Measure depth and front clearance before width. Width is the number people check; depth is the number that stops the door opening.

The short version

No single machine wins this comparison. The question is which compromise you can live with: less width for two drawers, or less drawer for a door.
