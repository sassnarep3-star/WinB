How to judge thermometer accuracy without taking the marketing at face value

Every thermometer listing claims accuracy. Here is how to read the claim properly, and how to check the device.

Published 2026-11-20 · 5 minute read · Question-led guide, not a product test

Thermometer listings are unusually good at accuracy claims and unusually bad at explaining them. Here is how to read one, and a two-minute check you can run the day yours arrives.

What the accuracy claim actually says

  • The plus or minus figure is a tolerance around a stated reading, not a guarantee about your particular piece of food.
  • It applies over a stated range, and that range may not include the temperature you care about.
  • Response speed is a separate number, and a fast wrong reading is still wrong.
  • A certification named on a listing title tells you a body certified something. It does not tell you which model, which batch, or when.

The ThermoMaven listing, for example, carries a 1 second response time, a plus or minus 0.5F figure, a NIST&NSF certification named on the title, and an IP67 water-resistance rating. Those are four different kinds of claim on one page, and they are worth separating before you compare them with a competitor's page.

The home check that takes two minutes

Fill a cup with ice and water, stir it, and plunge the probe in. Well-mixed ice water sits at 32F / 0C. Wait for the reading to settle and see where it lands.

  • Within about plus or minus 2F of 32F, the device is doing what its claim says.
  • Read well outside that, the device is out of tolerance and you should return it.
  • Read low and then slowly climbing, the probe may not have reached the water yet. Wait longer.
  • Read wildly high, check for condensation on the probe tip and dry it before trying again.

This is a consistency check, not a laboratory calibration. It tells you the device agrees with a known reference point, which is exactly what you need to know before you rely on it.

Why you would want a leave-in probe instead

A leave-in probe can also be checked in the same way, but it earns its price in a different place: it keeps measuring. The CHEF iQ Sense Gen3 listing states three ultra-thin probes, five sensors, 1000F heat-safe probes and WiFi with unlimited range, which means it is reporting continuously rather than only when you ask.

A note on probe count

More probes is not automatically better. Two probes in a large roast can tell you that the two sides are at different temperatures, which is genuinely useful. Three probes in three small pieces tells you the same thing at three times the cost. Match the probe count to the number of separate items you cook, not to the largest number on the box.

Next step

Decide what you are actually trying to control - the endpoint of a steak, or the shape of a long roast - then pick the simplest device that answers that question. Run the ice-water check on day one, and again if the readings ever look wrong.
