Format · Air fryers & countertop convection

Basket or oven-style air fryer: what should you compare?

Two formats, two different sets of trade-offs. The useful comparison is not which one is better, but which one fits how you cook.

Published 2026-11-20 · 4 minute read · Question-led guide, not a product test

The most common way people get an air fryer wrong is by picking a format before picking a food. A drawer-style fryer pulls a basket out towards you. An oven-style fryer opens like a small oven and you slide a shelf in. Both are called air fryers. They behave differently in a kitchen that is small, busy, or shared.

Start with access, not capacity

The headline number on the box tells you very little. What changes your day is whether you can see the food, get a spoon under it, and pull it out without tilting a hot basket over a pan. Drawer-style fryers give you the easiest access to the food itself, because the whole cooking chamber tilts out towards you. Oven-style fryers give you a level shelf and a hinged door, which is a better shape for a flat tray, a whole chicken, or a baking sheet.

Look at what the manufacturer actually ships in the box, because accessories drive this more than the marketing does. A basket that sits on a sliding drawer and a set of wire racks that slide onto a shelf are different objects, and they will not fit the same pot, the same cabinet, or the same dishwasher.

The questions worth asking

  • How much of the cooking chamber can you actually see, and do you need to?
  • Do you cook flat things - a tray of vegetables, a fillet, a sheet pan - or mostly loose, tossed food?
  • Is there a risk of tipping a hot basket over a hob, and does that matter in your kitchen?
  • Where will this live, and does a drawer-style unit need a wider clear space than its footprint suggests?
  • How many removable parts are there, and where do they all go when they are wet?

How to read a capacity number

A capacity figure is only meaningful next to a unit. Six quarts might be two three-quart baskets, which is a very different proposition from one six-quart basket, because the second lets you run two foods at once and finish them together.

The next thing to check is the usable cooking area rather than the total volume, and then whether the food you cook fits in one layer. A fryer that holds a stated number of pounds is telling you about total volume, not about whether your chicken breast lies flat. If a listing does not publish dimensions, treat them as unknown and measure the space in your own kitchen before you commit.

Next step

Measure the space you have, write down the two or three dishes you cook most, then check those against the exact manufacturer specifications for the models you are considering. The comparison that matters is the one between your kitchen and a specific model, not between two models in the abstract.
