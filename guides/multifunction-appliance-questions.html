Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Every extra function is a promise about a mode you will use twice. Five questions that separate a useful multi-function appliance from a crowded control panel.

By The Crisp Kitchen Editorial Desk · 2026-11-20 · 6 min read Topics are working hypotheses, not keyword data

A multi-function appliance makes a promise about modes. The useful question is not "how many functions" but "which of these will I use in a normal week, and what did I give up to get the rest".

The five questions

1. Which two functions will you actually use?

Name them before you look at the price. If you cannot name two, you are buying a control panel. A 24-preset oven is excellent if you want presets and no better than a dial if you will only ever use one.

2. What did the extra functions cost in physical space?

More modes usually means more interior volume, more accessories, or both. The cleanup guide covers how to measure that cost before you commit.

3. Does the appliance do the second job properly, or adequately?

An air fryer that also dehydrates is an air fryer with a low fan setting. That is a real function and a limited one. Ask what the temperature range and time limit are for the secondary mode, and whether they suit the job you have in mind.

4. What is the failure mode of the extra part?

Doors, hinges, baskets, probes and app connections all add a thing that can fail. A thermometer with an app is a thermometer plus a phone plus a network. When one of those three is unhappy, the cooking tool is still a thermometer — but a multi-function oven with a failed door is a cabinet.

5. What happens when the app is discontinued?

For connected appliances, ask whether the device works standalone. The CHEF iQ listing sells a WiFi probe system; the ThermoMaven listing sells a screen-on-the-device probe. The second one cannot be bricked by an app update. Neither listing states a support commitment, and this site does not assert one.

On claims in this category

Multi-function listings lean on certification and accuracy language: NIST, NSF, ±0.5°F, 1000°F safe. Those are seller claims in the listing text. This site repeats them only with that label, because the certifying bodies' registries were not checked for this build. See FLG-005.
