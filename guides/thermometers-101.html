How to use a thermometer so you never guess again

Where you probe matters as much as what you probe. The USDA numbers are simple; the placement is where dinner is won.

By the Counterwise Editorial Desk · 2026-11-20 · 3 min read

A thermometer's job is not to impress you with a number; it is to end the guess. The guess it ends is specific: is this safe to serve yet? The answers are government-published, short, and worth memorizing in the same way you memorized the smoke-detector chirp.

The numbers, from USDA FSIS

Minimum internal temperatures, measured with a food thermometer:

  • 165 °F — all poultry: breasts, whole birds, legs, thighs, wings, ground poultry, giblets, and stuffing.
  • 145 °F with a 3-minute rest — whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, and lamb (steaks, chops, roasts). The rest is part of the kill step; set the meat down and let the clock run.
  • 160 °F — all ground meats (beef, pork, lamb, veal). Grinding sends surface bacteria into the middle, which is exactly why the burger rule differs from the steak rule.
  • 165 °F — leftovers, reheated. One more time, all the way through.

Doneness is personal; safety is not. Cooking a chop to 150 because you like it pinker than medium is fine — the standard is a floor, never a ceiling. (All of the above from the FSIS "Steps to Keep Food Safe" and "Food Thermometers" pages, usda.gov.)

Placement is the skill

  • Thickest part, away from bone. Bone conducts heat; a probe touching it reads high and pulls the steak off early. A thermometer that reads 4 degrees hot is a dinner that reads like sand — accuracy claims on packaging don't survive bad placement.
  • Whole birds: three checks. FSIS advises the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing, and the thickest part of the breast, avoiding bone in all three. The bird is done when all three clear 165; the breast alone doesn't speak for the thigh.
  • Thin things get angled. A cutlet or a fillet doesn't have depth for a straight insert; slide the probe in from the side so the sensing tip sits in the geometric center.

Instant-read vs leave-in, in one paragraph

An instant-read answers "is it there yet?" — open, probe, read, close; seconds per check, and the right tool for a dozen chicken thighs in an air fryer. A leave-in probe answers "tell me when it's there" — it sits in a long roast and alerts you while you do something else. They are different jobs; a household usually needs the first one before the second one.

On accuracy claims
Listing pages on our shop shelf show ±0.5°F-type claims and certification marks; we treat those as the seller's words, not verified measurements — the link and capture date are in the register. If a number matters for safety, test the device: ice water reads 32 °F, boiling water reads 212 °F at sea level. A probe off by 8 degrees is a cooking habit you never chose.
