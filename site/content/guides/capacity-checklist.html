A capacity number is the loudest figure on a product page and the least self-explanatory. "8 qt" tells you the interior volume; it does not tell you whether your weeknight chicken thighs fit in a single layer, which is the only arrangement where air frying does what it promises.

The checklist

  • Single-layer test. Lay your most common main course (not the package photo — your actual portion) flat on a counter. Now compare its footprint to the basket's usable flat area as printed in the listing or manual. If the food must stack, expect to shake, rotate, and batch.
  • Batch math beats total volume. Divide: if a full dinner is three single-layer rounds, the real question is whether rounds of eight minutes feel fast or annoying. Two smaller chambers that run in parallel can out-produce one large one at the same wall socket.
  • Shape literacy. A deep round basket holds drumsticks beautifully and wastes corner space on fries. A shallow wide tray is the reverse. Match the basket's shape to your food's shape — this comparison usually matters more than a one-quart difference.
  • The accessories asterisk. Some listings count a crisper plate's displacement inside the stated volume; accessories, rotisserie rods, and multi-rack claims change usable area. The listing's "What's included" section is the record; the manual is the tiebreaker.
  • Write your number down. Decide before you shop: "a single layer of wings for N people" is a specification. "Looks big in photos" is how returns happen.

What we deliberately don't publish

No quart-to-plate conversions and no "best for families" ratings. Those charts circulate widely without measurement behind them, and every appliance is different inside. Verify the exact model's printed dimensions at the retailer and compare them to the food you actually cook.
